Project Refinement: Heritage Vault 2.0 Proposal

Original project: In IT 370 (Linux Administration), I wrote a technical implementation proposal for FamilySearch's simulated "Heritage Vault 2.0" modernization. The original draft addressed high-volume access, security, storage, and performance using HAProxy, SELinux, ZFS, and Keepalived — but treated each requirement as a purely technical exercise, without asking who could be harmed by the design.

The refinement I made: I revisited that specific proposal and changed seven concrete elements of it, driven by one question: how do we scale to billions of records while protecting the privacy of living individuals and the sanctity of temple ordinance data? The table below documents each change, not a generic checklist.

Area of the Heritage Vault ProposalOriginal DesignRefined DesignEthical Reason
User access tiers (Section 1) Single permission model for all users Three tiers: church-member accounts with ordinance access; professional genealogist accounts with API/bulk access; researcher accounts scoped to aggregated historical data with living-person records excluded Least privilege applied to end users, not just administrators. Protects living individuals' privacy and restricts sacred ordinance records to those with a legitimate claim to them.
ZFS dataset structure (Section 3) One pool for all genealogical data Separate datasets for public records, document images, and restricted ordinance records — each with its own quota, compression, and read-only policy An administrator managing public data should never accidentally reach sacred records. Structural separation enforces the boundary technically rather than by policy alone.
Encryption scope (Section 2) Volume-level encryption (LUKS) only Volume-level LUKS plus file-level encryption for temple ordinance records specifically If storage media is ever physically compromised, the most sensitive records remain unreadable even to someone with the volume passphrase.
Load balancer routing (Section 1) HAProxy distributing all traffic across one backend pool Dedicated backend pools for API/bulk-query traffic, separate from casual user traffic Fairness: a professional genealogist running bulk exports should not degrade response times for a church member searching for a family name.
CDN coverage (Section 1) CDN for static assets only, justified on latency grounds CDN explicitly justified on accessibility grounds — serving users on low-bandwidth connections in remote regions Equitable access to family history records is a matter of fairness, not only performance. Users with slower connections deserve the same experience.
Access auditing (Section 2) AIDE file-integrity monitoring on system files AIDE retained, plus access auditing on any dataset containing ordinance records, so that even administrator access is logged Broad access — especially admin access to sacred data — requires accountability. The audit trail protects both the records and the people trusted with them.
Implementation sequencing (Section 5) Four phases treated as roughly equal priorities Security foundation (SELinux, RBAC, tiered permissions) explicitly prioritized as Phase 1, ahead of storage and availability Stewardship means protecting people first. A highly available system that leaks private records is a failure, not a success.
What this refinement taught me

The original proposal was technically competent but ethically silent — it optimized for uptime and scalability without asking who could be harmed. Changing seven specific elements of one specific proposal forced me to apply Stewardship and Integrity to the document itself, not just to a set of abstract principles. The most important lesson: ethics is not a section you add at the end of a proposal. It is a question you ask at the start of every design decision.
